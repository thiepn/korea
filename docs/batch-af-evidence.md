## Chapter III.5 — Governing the Three Kingdoms and Gaya

**Grounded institutional references**

1. **National Institute of Korean History (NIKH)**, *Revised Korean History*, *Goguryeo political and economic society*, central council and aristocratic government: https://db.history.go.kr/diachronic/level.do?levelId=nh_005_0050_0010_0020_0020 . Specialists distinguish early `jega` associations from the changing noble council and the political competition surrounding later `daedaero` office. This source does not validate a timeless unchanged council across five centuries.
2. **NIKH**, *Revised Korean History*, *Baekje political and social organization*, central ranks, offices and noble meeting institutions: https://db.history.go.kr/diachronic/level.do?levelId=nh_006_0050_0010 . The evidence explicitly links rank, office and aristocratic council while describing changes across Hanseong, Ungjin and Sabi. Six `jwapyeong`, sixteen ranks and twenty-two `bu` are **different historical classifications**, not interchangeable numbers.
3. **NIKH**, *Three Kingdoms organization and centralization*: https://contents.history.go.kr/front/ta/view.do?levelId=ta_h61_0040_0020_0050 . Useful pedagogic comparative synthesis: five `bu` in Goguryeo/Baekje, six in Silla; noble councils, ranked government and territorial expansion. Earlier state affiliations must not be conflated with later same-named administrative divisions.
4. **Academy of Korean Studies (AKS)**, *History of Korean bureaucracy*: https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0004813 . A diachronic reference for royal offices, departmental systems and later changes. The later institutional forms of Goryeo, Unified Silla and Joseon should **not** be projected backwards as if fully present during the early kingdom foundations.
5. **NIKH**, *A History of Korea*, chapter on Three Kingdoms political and social structures: https://contents.history.go.kr/resources/common/pdf/A%20History%20of%20Korea_The%20Understanding%20Korea%20Series.pdf . English synthesis, especially useful for terminology, early aristocratic power, ranks and comparative variation. It is not a primary transcript of an early council.

**Evidence limits and contested reconstructions**

- `Samguk sagi` is a twelfth-century historical compilation drawing on earlier materials and imposing a dynastic chronology; the earliest royal origin stories and foundation years are not uniformly supported by contemporary documentation. A sacred origin narrative can be historically important as **memory and legitimacy** without serving as contemporary registration of a birth or kingship.
- King's court titles, noble rank and actual office function must be separated. `bu` can change meaning over time. The Goguryeo `jega`, Baekje `jeongsaam` and Silla `hwabaek` were **not equivalent modern parliaments** or identical institutions. Sources concerning procedure and purported unanimity do not warrant invented minutes or electorates.
- Sixth-century Silla stone inscriptions and Goguryeo's Gwanggaeto stele offer important original-language evidence for authority, but commemorative statements of conquest are political claims, not independently measured long-term territorial control. Burials and fortresses reveal material investment but cannot map every official's jurisdiction or identify every gravesite's ethnicity.
- Laws attributed to Goguryeo Sosurim (373) and Silla Beopheung (520) are transmitted reform chronology, **not complete surviving early law codes**; enforcement, penalties and household rights remain underdocumented. Rank restrictions and gendered eligibility changed and should not be generalized across all groups and centuries.
- **Gaya is plural**: Geumgwan Gaya and Daegaya must not be reduced to a failed singular monarchy or retrofitted into Silla's bone-rank rules. Early Gaya is discussed only through cross-state institutional comparison. Full Gaya history remains in III.4.
- **Ownership**: III.5 covers constitutional and social institutions of governance across Three Kingdoms and Gaya (royal succession, elite selection, councils, official ranking, territorial administration, law, revenue, capital geography and comparison). Kingdom-specific campaigns, territorial changes and ruler biographies belong to III.1–III.4. Post-668 Unified Silla administration and Gyeongju government belong to IV.2. Northern Buyeo/Okjeo/Dongye institutional histories belong to II.4.

## Chapter VI.6 — Early Joseon land and productive economy

**Specific historical and scholarly references**

1. **NIKH**, *Revised Korean History*, *The Economic Structure of Early Joseon*, https://db.history.go.kr/id/nh_024_0010 . Specialist survey of **1391 Gwajeonbeop**, land tenure and revenue entitlements, farmland ownership, agrarian productivity, `gongbeop`, `gongmul`, `yoyeok`, distribution, state finances and regional institutions. Do not use later `Daedongbeop` as a synonym for the early field-tax reform.
2. **AKS**, *Gwajeonbeop*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0004611 . Distinguishes grant of harvest-revenue collection rights from full private ownership. Tracks 1391 inception, changes in inspection, **1466 Jikjeonbeop** and **1470 government collection/distribution**; avoid conflating official emoluments with individual absolute land title.
3. **NIKH**, *Sejong's agricultural directive and Nongsa jikseol*, https://contents.history.go.kr/front/hm/view.do?levelId=hm_082_0010&tabId=03&treeId=010502 . Highlights the collection of experienced farmers' methods (especially three southern provinces) and 1429 compilation; sources are compiled advice rather than measured nationwide yield increases.
4. **AKS**, *Gongbeop*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0004304 . Defines **six classes of field productivity and nine annual harvest classes**, promulgation **1444**, and subsequent **phased provincial rollout**, alongside debates about damage inspection and administration. A royal calendar date is not a claim of identical local implementation on that date.
5. **Historical Society of Korea**, *Household class system and tribute distribution from Taejo to Sejong*, https://journal.kci.go.kr/hksh/archive/articleView?artiId=ART002153110 . Peer-reviewed analysis of evolving classifications of household contribution; fiscal classification does not give complete demographic or labor information for every family.
6. **AKS**, *Jangmun (Jangsi)*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0048454 . Early provincial periodic market emergence in Jeolla famine conditions, with the **1473** debate; widespread standardized five-day market systems grew later. Do not claim full five-day-market networks existed nationwide in 1392.
7. **AKS**, *Jongnu commercial streets*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0052922 . Early Hanseong planned `sijeon` market rows built in the fifteenth-century capital; a sijeon shop is not identical to a provincial jangsi or later market privileges.
8. **AKS**, *Gyeonggongjang* and *Gongjang*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0002288 and https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0004428 . Capital and provincial artisans, occupational registers and compulsory service. A later listed establishment is not necessarily the exact scope of the system during the founding decade.
9. **AKS**, Joseon salt production and intervention, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0051904 . Early use of boiling concentrated seawater, government supply concerns, and the short-lived strengthened salt restriction in **1445**; not modern industrial solar salt evaporation.
10. **AKS**, *Jo-un grain transport*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0052436 . Government grain ships, coastal and river logistics, warehouses and labor; later detailed departure dates and fleet-size categories cannot be read backward uncritically. Its evidence does not establish absence of damage, spoilage or unrecorded transport cost.
11. **AKS**, *Market history and official money*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0032448 . Early Joseon `jeohwa`, `Joseon tongbo`, rice/cloth exchange, limited circulation and later monetization. An official issue is not proof of national adoption.

**Evidence limits and interpretive care**

- Distinguish **land ownership, labor of cultivation, right to receive produce, public land tax, tribute, military support and corvée**. These were multiple legally and economically different claims; a single tax percentage cannot describe the total burden.
- `Gongbeop` (fifteenth-century field taxation) is not `gongmul` (tribute goods) and not much later `Daedongbeop` (later tribute reform). `Jeonbun yukdeung` and `yeonbun gudeung` encode assessment categories, not modern universal yield statistics. A `gyeol` is not an unvarying hectare.
- No full nationwide series of actual rent contracts, crop yields, households, merchant prices, mine output or salt pans exists for every early Joseon district. Regulations describe a program; they **do not** directly prove rural compliance or equal protection. Cautions about modern monetary equivalence are deliberate.
- Granary relief instructions and local practices need separate evaluation: a surviving royal directive does not document the experience of each beneficiary. The roles of women and laborers can be described structurally where evidence warrants, but invented household quotations are prohibited.
- **Ownership:** VI.6 covers early Joseon land grants and tenure, agronomy, waterworks, assessment, tribute/labor, public finance, early market forms, craft production and commodity movement. V.4 owns Goryeo land and economy; VI.7 owns diplomatic relations as foreign policy; VII.6 owns later Joseon agrarian commercialization, Daedongbeop-era market shifts and mature five-day-market networks. VI.4 owns Hangul, not agricultural literacy.
