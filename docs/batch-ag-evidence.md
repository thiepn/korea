## II.6 — Mahan and the Rise of Baekje

**Authoritative and scholarly entry points**

1. **National Institute of Korean History**, *Baekje-guk and Mokji-guk* (part of specialist Baekje political-history chapters), https://db.history.go.kr/diachronic/level.do?levelId=nh_006_0020_0020_0010. Its primary-reference analysis documents the **third-century textual horizon** of Baekje-guk in the `Sanguozhi`, and the reconstructed interaction of Baekje with Mokji-guk and wider Mahan polities. It explicitly questions equating all early Mahan groups with a single royal opponent in the `Samguk sagi` founder story.
2. **National Institute of Korean History**, *Baekje formation and development*, https://contents.history.go.kr/front/nh/print.do?levelId=nh_006_0020_0020&whereStr= . Contains archaeologically grounded discussion of Garak, Seokchon, Mongchon, early Baekje and the varying evidentiary status of retrospective founder campaigns.
3. **Korean Citation Index**, scholarly archaeology of Mahan-to-Baekje transition in the **Chungju/Namhan** area, https://kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch/ciSereArtiView.kci?sereArticleSearchBean.artiId=ART003110955 . The published abstract identifies separate **pre-mid-fourth-century Mahan**, **mid/late-fourth-century transition** and **late-fourth-century Baekje consolidation** phases in this *specific region*, including ironworking, settlements, tombs and fortresses. These timings cannot be applied wholesale to Jeolla, Hanseong or all Mahan polities.
4. **Academy of Korean Studies**, *Han River*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0060950 . Historical and geographical analysis of Han basin settlement and defensive sites, including alternative identifications for early Baekje's royal center. Excavation and site identifications remain open to reassessment.
5. **UNESCO World Heritage Centre**, *Baekje Historic Areas*, https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1477/ . The eight inscribed archaeological sites primarily evidence the **later Baekje period, 475–660**, associated with Ungjin and Sabi. They should not be cited as if they directly prove the 18 BCE foundation, Mahan festivals, or third-century political offices.

**Method, chronology and interpretation**

- Mahan is the term used in external sources for **multiple western/southern peninsular polities**, not a single perfectly bounded modern territorial state. `Guo`, `king`, `chief`, `Jin king` and related titles must be interpreted within the outside compiler's political terminology; no complete local administrative archive survives.
- `Sanguozhi` is a third-century Chinese compilation, while `Samguk sagi` was compiled in the twelfth century. Reports in the latter of **Onjo and the 18 BCE traditional foundation** carry historical significance as later political memory, but do **not** constitute independent first-century BCE eyewitness evidence.
- The `Biryu`/`Onjo` traditions, ties to Goguryeo founders, `Wiryeseong` site associations, and the Mokji/Baekje contest should be discussed as separate questions; modern archaeological fortifications do not come labeled with the founder's year or the name of their builder.
- Excavated graves, crafts and houses can show differences in social investment, burial practice and trade. One funerary vessel cannot prove ethnic identity, exact political affiliation or a named official post. A distinctive material style can continue through changing sovereignty.
- Coastal and river corridors make mobility plausible, but no surviving complete census provides population counts, uniform crop yield, annual Mahan taxation or fleet sizes. Trade origin and political control should be inferred only with explicit limitations.
- **Ownership:** II.6 owns Mahan records, geography, social organization, ritual, Onjo foundation traditions, early Han archaeology and Baekje's *formation*. II.3 owns Han commandery government; II.4 owns Buyeo, Okjeo and Dongye; II.7/II.8 separately own Jinhan/Silla and Byeonhan/Gaya. Part III owns later Baekje reigns, campaigns, mature royal office, law and government. Do not duplicate later Baekje monumental history in 475–660 UNESCO sites.

## IV.7 — Maritime Korea in the eighth and ninth centuries

**Institutional and documentary sources**

1. **National Institute of Korean History**, *Silla commerce and Tang–Japan maritime interactions*, https://contents.history.go.kr/front/nh/view.do?levelId=nh_009_0050_0020_0030 . Discusses ninth-century Silla resident merchant communities, `Silla-bang`, Tang transport corridors and the **840 Hoeyeoksa case**. The Japanese court rejected a Jang Bogo agent's status as a formal royal envoy while allowing some transactions; refusal of diplomatic accreditation is **not** proof of a total trade ban.
2. **NIKH**, *Jang Bogo and Cheonghaejin*, https://contents.history.go.kr/front/nh/view.do?levelId=nh_011_0030_0030 . Specialist history of Wando's strategic maritime installation, Jang Bogo's Tang experience, Chishan patronage, network organization, involvement in Silla succession and the center's decline.
3. **Academy of Korean Studies**, *Jang Bogo*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0048474 , and *Cheonghaejin*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0056856 . Historical account of **828 establishment**, conventional **846 assassination** and **851 dissolution/relocation**. Other transmitted summaries put the assassination in **848**; the conflict is openly documented rather than silently harmonized.
4. **AKS**, *Wando Cheonghaejin archaeological remains*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0048392 . Excavated ramparts and timber defenses demonstrate investment at a fortified coastal center; they do not identify every sailor, patrol route or the precise social costs of the garrison.
5. **AKS**, *Hoeyeoksa*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0065541 , and *Gyeondang Maemulsa*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0002150 . Specialist distinction between Jang Bogo's commercial missions to Japan or Tang and the Silla king's official diplomatic missions. The **840** Hoeyeoksa mission is documented, not a universal description of all maritime transactions.
6. **AKS**, *Silla-so* and *Silla-gwan*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0032835 and https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0032807 . Overseas community infrastructure described through selected literary and travel accounts. These were not independent sovereign colonial districts or modern consulates.
7. **AKS**, *Silla Buddhist temple communities*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0032849 , and *Silla Buddhist ritual*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0032827 . The latter identifies observations by **Ennin in 839** at Chishan Beophwawon, recitation, chanting, lectures and hospitality. A ceremony in Tang territory is not evidence that every monastery in Silla conducted identical ritual.
8. **AKS**, *Balhae*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0021626 . Describes Balhae's own diplomatic and commercial contacts with Japan: the published summary reports **35 Balhae missions to Japan and 13 in the reverse direction**, mostly official encounters. These numbers do **not** measure private merchant trips or ship traffic, and the routes and incentives differed from Silla networks.
9. **AKS**, *Yi Won-tae*, https://encykorea.aks.ac.kr/Article/E0045386 . Documents the hazardous **786 Balhae-to-Japan journey** with drift, casualties and return logistics. It is a specific documented episode, not a statistically representative shipwreck rate.
10. **NIKH**, *Jang Bogo historical chronology*, https://contents.history.go.kr/mobile/kc/view.do?levelId=kc_n101630 . Separates the central **838–839 royal succession** involvement from trade, and also reflects the conventional 846 death date.

**Comparative source limitations**

- Sea routes are reconstructed from known ports and texts; no historical voyage supplies a continuous modern GPS track, standardized journey-time distribution, insurance scheme, fleet-wide manifest or exact ship capacity.
- Tang residents categorized as Silla people worked within Tang legal jurisdictions; `Silla-bang` and `Silla-so` are *not* independent overseas provinces of the Silla monarchy. The diary of Ennin supplies unusually direct evidence for selected encounters, not a comprehensive community census.
- Balhae's Japanese embassies should **not** be merged with Silla's commercial networks. Their formal status, northern geography, diplomatic and material exchange conditions are different.
- A cargo including imported objects from western or southern Asia does not prove that a Silla-owned ship traveled directly to the original production area. Separate origin of goods, place of purchase, route of transport and later depositional context.
- Threats of raiding and slave-taking were consequential, but we have no comprehensive victim list, numerical incident rate or uniform legal definition of piracy. Protection could also create concentrated private coercive power.
- Official 851 abolition and relocation of residents should be distinguished from archaeological occupation phases and the survival of merchant relationships. Jang Bogo's actions are not a substitute for the skills and labor of anonymous crews and shore communities.
- **Ownership:** IV.7 is commercial and maritime history of the eighth and ninth centuries. IV.5 owns Balhae founding and state diplomacy; IV.6 owns Balhae society and urban archaeology; IV.8 owns broader late-Silla breakdown, elite factionalism and rural unrest; IV.9/IV.10 own later reunification and Goryeo's beginning. Jang Bogo's political involvement appears only insofar as it affected the Cheonghaejin maritime network.
