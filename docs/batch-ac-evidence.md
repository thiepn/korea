## Chapter I.5: material and chronological evidence

- **National Museum of Korea**, Daho-ri Tomb 1 and visitors' historical trail: https://www.museum.go.kr/ENG/contents/E0201020000.do?relicRecommendPk=PS01001001_58&schM=list . Gives a concrete late-first-century BCE wood-coffin assemblage including iron weapons and agricultural tools, bronze and lacquered items, Han mirrors and coins. **An elite funerary selection is not a census of everyday ownership, a foundry location or proof of annual trade volume.**
- **Daegu National Museum**, *Ancient Korean Kingdoms*: https://daegu.museum.go.kr/eng/sub02_01_01_03.do . Provides museum periodization and evidence that iron objects circulated from about the third century BCE and broadly increased thereafter, with subsequent regional transitions toward Samhan, Silla, Gaya and Baekje. Museum periodizations cannot date all regional adoption to a single year.
- **National Museum of Korea**, *Metal, Iron and Steel — The Cultural History of Iron*: https://www.museum.go.kr/ENG/contents/E0202010000.do?exhiSpThemId=174666&listType=&menuId=current&schM=view . Curatorial synthesis of material production, uses and the relationship between iron and power. Such an exhibition does not independently attest the legal ownership of mines or a uniform industry-wide workforce.
- **National Museum of Korea**, *Korean-type Bronze Dagger: Emblem of a Ruler*: https://www.museum.go.kr/ENG/contents/E0401000000.do?relicRecommendId=519718&schM=view . Demonstrates why bronze ritual and elite display coexisted with later increasing iron, and why period labels cannot imply instantaneous material replacement.
- **National Museum of Korea**, *Gaya Confederacy*: https://www.museum.go.kr/ENG/contents/E0201030700.do?showHallId=760&showroomCode=DM0007 . Contextual reference for iron resources along the Nakdong and later commercial relations with Nangnang and the Japanese archipelago. The later mature Gaya economy is **not** assigned to every earlier Byeonhan or prehistoric site.

**Interpretation boundaries:** I.5 treats the economic consequences and materially evidenced networks of early iron, not the political narrative of iron-using historical states. Smelting slag, smithing debris, finished imports, repaired implements and burial goods carry different evidentiary implications. Do not treat coins in graves as proof of universal monetization; similar metal objects as a dated shipping manifest; the movement of technology as direct state occupation; or changes in farming as automatic products of iron. Specific Liaodong and Han-commandery political institutions belong to II.2/II.3; formal Baekje, Silla, Gaya and Goguryeo state histories to their assigned Chapters; archaeological settlement, social identity and chiefly polities to the relevant other Part I Chapters. Attributing named voices to unrecorded craftspeople or farm households is prohibited.

## Chapter IV.2: royal institutions, local governance, and primary record limitations

- **National Institute of Korean History**, *A History of Korea: The Understanding Korea Series*: https://contents.history.go.kr/resources/common/pdf/A%20History%20of%20Korea_The%20Understanding%20Korea%20Series.pdf . Institutional account of Jipsabu, specialist departments, Gukhak, the nine provinces and five secondary capitals, inherited aristocratic distinctions and royal-period changes. The compilation is modern synthesis, not a seventh-century official minute book.
- **Korean Citation Index**, *Local Administration Reorganization after the Unification of the Three Kingdoms*: https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch/ciSereArtiView.kci?sereArticleSearchBean.artiId=ART002472164 . The key historiographical correction is that local government after unification developed from earlier institutional forms, not in one miraculous administrative act following the war. Formal ranks held by provincial notables did not create equal access to central bone-rank privileges.
- **Korean Citation Index**, *Military-Geographical Relations of the Nine Provinces, Five Secondary Capitals and Ten Garrison Units*: https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch/ciSereArtiView.kci?sereArticleSearchBean.artiId=ART003117234 . Specialized analysis of the geographic link between provincial centers, military stations and transport. A mapped headquarters does not establish permanent effective rule across every neighboring area.
- **Okayama University**, *Proposed Locations of Villages Recorded in the Silla Village Register*: https://ousar.lib.okayama-u.ac.jp/ja/70053 . Shows live disagreement and examination of topography, historic place names and the locations of the four villages in the surviving document. **A four-village register is not a national census**, nor a complete tax ledger.
- **UNESCO World Heritage Centre**, *Gyeongju Historic Areas*: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/976/ . Evidence for Wolseong, religious, elite burial, mountain and fortress landscapes in the old capital. Individual material features must be phased and dated; do not infer a specific court decision directly from a palace wall.
- **National Museum of Korea**, *Unified Silla Period*: https://www.museum.go.kr/ENG/contents/E0201030900.do?showHallId=760&showroomCode=DM0034 . Visual material and international exchange context, but not a complete inventory of government ranks or ordinary population views.

**Interpretation boundaries:** IV.2 owns mature Unified Silla monarchy, Jipsabu, Hwabaek, bone rank, provinces, five secondary capitals, lower administration, Silla Village Register, fiscal government, defense and Gyeongju as political center. IV.1 already owns the immediate 668–698 human and geopolitical settlement. IV.3 owns everyday social life, IV.4 religious culture, IV.5 Balhae political history; later Silla disintegration belongs to its designated Chapter. Do not describe Hwabaek as representative democracy, equate named aristocratic ranks with identifiable burial objects, interpret official revenue assignments as modern absolute land ownership, assume every locality kept identical records or date the entire mature governmental chart to 676. Different royal reigns and revisions are treated in their respective chronology.
