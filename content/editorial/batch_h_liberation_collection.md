# Batch H: After Liberation — Local Politics, Occupation, and Divided Sovereignty

These original essays complete the unpublished portion of IX.1. Their central question is how an enormous change in formal sovereignty became a contested set of local, economic, and international practices. The narrative does not manufacture testimony, archival quotations, or certainty from gaps in evidence.

## IX.1.4
### Who kept the lights on after an empire fell?
The first problem of liberation was practical before it could be constitutional. On a street where people had assembled to celebrate the end of Japanese rule, someone still had to decide who could open the storehouse, organize a watch, authorize a delivery, and prevent a departing official from disposing of public property. Colonial bureaucracy did not vanish cleanly on a single August day. It left buildings, registers, police stations, tax demands, railway timetables, and people trained to operate them. At the same time, it lost the political claim that had sustained its right to command. That mismatch between surviving administration and collapsed legitimacy made the local committee far more than an improvised political club. It was one answer to the immediate question of who could act in public without waiting for distant authorities.

Early organizers drew on neighborhood associations, independence circles, workers' networks, religious contacts, and people with experience in colonial offices. Their backgrounds were neither identical nor consistently aligned. A town could bring together merchants concerned about disorder, activists expecting social reform, teachers seeking Korean control over schools, and farmers who wondered whether landlords' powers would change. The same committee name could therefore hide different social coalitions. Describing all these groups as already divided into the later northern and southern regimes strips the moment of its genuine uncertainty. In late summer 1945, the future state was not yet a settled reality; membership, influence, and even the acceptable definition of independence were being contested locally.

### Representation was a claim, not a settled fact
The spread of people's committees gave a visible form to the argument that the population should govern itself. But an organization calling itself a representative body had still to answer difficult questions. Who selected its officers? Could villagers remove them? Did women have practical access to meetings? Were former colonial collaborators excluded, reconciled, or quietly retained because they knew how administration worked? Which groups could speak without intimidation? Historians must separate the documented existence of committees from the much harder task of establishing the social depth of their legitimacy. It is possible for an organization to represent an important popular demand and for its internal practices to be narrow at the same time.

The arrival of occupation authorities exposed that ambiguity. Some northern committees could be incorporated into the emerging occupation-sponsored political system, though not without alteration. In the southern zone, US military authorities did not recognize the People's Republic as a lawful sovereign government. Local organizations faced pressure from rival parties, administrative decisions, and competing claims over police and property. Their subsequent histories cannot be explained by a single verdict about whether they were spontaneous or controlled. What mattered was the interaction of immediate local needs with increasingly powerful institutions able to recognize, reshape, or suppress them.

### The politics of an office doorway
A committee's influence became real when a resident could bring a complaint to its door and expect an answer. The questions might concern a food distribution, a factory wage, the removal of a hated policeman, or a family returning to a house now occupied by someone else. Each decision could change the social position of neighbors. A promise of independence mattered differently to the employer, the tenant, the returning conscript, and the person whose work had been indispensable to a colonial office. Once liberation turned these inequalities into public arguments, political legitimacy depended increasingly on competence and fairness in ordinary transactions. The broad story of partition is incomplete without this local struggle to define trustworthy authority.

Documents about committees often survive because an official wanted to count, praise, monitor, or discredit them. Those purposes shape what appears on paper. An occupation report may describe a security problem; a party history may describe a revolutionary awakening; a local memoir may remember confusion and relief. Reading these genres against one another does not produce a perfectly neutral account, but it can prevent a single institutional voice from becoming the entire history. The strength of a local committee is thus measured not only by the number of meetings it held, but by the contested records of what it actually managed to do.

## IX.1.5
### A northern occupation and the uncertainty of power
Soviet entry into northern Korea was a military event with political consequences that continued to develop long after the first troops crossed into cities. It did not instantaneously produce the institutions and leadership known from later decades. Existing Korean activists, local self-governing organizations, nationalist figures, and groups with communist commitments all operated within a space in which the occupying command possessed decisive leverage. Early administrative improvisations mattered because there was no ready-made national state waiting behind the Japanese authorities. Decisions about police, transport, industry, and food distribution helped determine who could claim to govern, and in whose name.

The occupation authorities did not treat local Korean organizations merely as scenery. They relied on them for information and administration while also selecting which political initiatives to encourage. The relationship was therefore asymmetrical but not empty of local agency. Korean leaders argued over personnel, land, public order, and political strategy. Nationalists who hoped to protect religious, commercial, or community interests did not necessarily share the priorities of communist organizers. For a time, committees with mixed backgrounds could hold space that later disappeared. Looking backward from the established North Korean state risks turning a fluid occupation into a story with a predetermined ending.

### Administration begins to select winners
Institutions acquire power through appointments, budgets, security forces, and control of publication as much as through slogans. Northern arrangements developed through new provincial and regional bodies, and the occupation command retained a capacity to supervise and authorize. Decisions about the return of industrial enterprises to production or the organization of agricultural supply could strengthen one set of officials at the expense of another. Participation in a committee did not guarantee that its members retained influence when a higher authority standardized the administration. Nor did a change in name automatically mean the transformation of every practice beneath it; older technical routines often survived even when their political justification changed.

The story of northern political consolidation must include the difference between land as a national question and land as the livelihood of a household. Reform promised to alter relationships among landowners, tenants, village elites, and agricultural workers. It was also administered through institutions that could classify people, decide eligibility, and enforce new rules. For beneficiaries the change could be an emancipation from dependency; for others it could be dispossession or evidence that disagreement carried rising danger. A reader should be wary of narratives that permit only enthusiasm or only terror. Different communities and social groups encountered the transformation under different conditions, and state documents were particularly unlikely to preserve every dissenting experience.

### Kim Il Sung and a changing field of alternatives
Kim Il Sung's subsequent prominence makes it tempting to begin with him as though no alternative leadership mattered. Yet the occupation period contained established nationalists, veteran activists, Christian community figures, left-wing organizers, and competing networks. Their fortunes changed through bargaining, removal from office, organizational discipline, and the Soviet command's preferences. The later political language of a unified national revolutionary leadership should therefore be studied as a claim about authority produced by history, not assumed to describe the complex field of 1945 from the outset.

The 38th parallel also became increasingly material to northern political life. Families and businesses that once moved people and goods across the peninsula confronted new permits, checkpoints, interrupted commerce, and governments pursuing different economic arrangements. The separation was initially presented as a matter of military administration, but the machinery built inside each zone gave the line political weight. A government is not formed only when its establishment is proclaimed. It emerges in the cumulative ability to issue orders, collect resources, choose officials, and prevent other bodies from doing the same. That slower history helps explain how temporary occupation evolved toward distinct state structures.

## IX.1.6
### The southern military government inherits an empire's machinery
The United States Army Military Government in Korea was established south of the 38th parallel in September 1945 and governed until the inauguration of the Republic of Korea in August 1948. Its officers entered an administrative environment shaped by four decades of Japanese colonial rule. They encountered ministries with established files, police organizations, property records, courts, and technical officials whose expertise could appear essential to preventing breakdown. Reusing portions of that apparatus could produce continuity in daily services. It also provoked a legitimacy problem: Koreans who celebrated liberation could still find familiar faces issuing instructions from familiar desks. The resulting tensions were not peripheral public-relations difficulties. They reached into the credibility of the new order.

Military government differed from Korean sovereignty. Its authorities could appoint, promulgate regulations, and decide which political organizations were recognized as lawful. Yet the success of a particular directive depended on Korean interpreters, civil servants, police, merchants, farmers, and workers responding to it. Administrative control on paper did not eliminate the possibility of strikes, protests, rival organizations, or quiet evasion. The distance between a proclamation and its implementation was particularly wide when occupation officials lacked deep knowledge of Korean social and political divisions. The American command's need for rapid order encouraged reliance on established administrative capacity, just when part of the population demanded a decisive break with the colonial past.

### A language of order meets an argument about independence
The military government did not recognize the self-proclaimed People's Republic as the legitimate government of southern Korea. Korean political organizations nevertheless continued to compete over who represented the nation, and whose sacrifice under colonial rule gave them authority. This conflict was not simply between Americans and Koreans. Korean conservatives, left-wing activists, centrist negotiators, returning exiles, and local associations disagreed among themselves about the desired form of a new state. Occupation administrators also disagreed over practical policy and personnel. Political coalitions could change as one issue—trusteeship, labor rights, police reform, or elections—reordered the available alliances.

Administration increasingly involved Korean appointees, but the transfer of visible posts did not always transfer decisive authority. American officials continued to exercise final powers in finance, appointments, security, and property. The institutional history is therefore better followed through actual veto points than through ceremonial titles. Who could sign a decree? Who controlled the currency, rice procurement, or imported aid? Who could override a Korean official? A newspaper photograph showing a Korean administrator at a desk could signify change while concealing the hierarchy under which the desk operated. The distinction between participation and sovereignty is central to this period.

### Property, policing, and the price of expertise
Former Japanese-owned assets raised the question of what liberation meant in economic terms. Some property could not simply be assigned to an individual returning claimant because its ownership had been organized through corporations, colonial state agencies, or absentee interests. Control over these assets affected factories, housing, rents, transport, and patronage. The management of former colonial property was also a source of suspicion: who benefited from sales, appointments, or access? These issues connected high administrative decisions to street-level inequality. The same policy could be explained officially as necessary stabilization and denounced by opponents as reproducing colonial privilege.

Police reform revealed another severe contradiction. Officers who knew local terrain, files, and informant networks could help enforce new regulations, but associations with colonial repression made their continued presence deeply contested. Even a legitimate need for public safety did not answer who would be policed, on whose instructions, and with what safeguards. As organized political conflict intensified, policing became part of the struggle rather than a neutral service located outside it. Describing the period as straightforward democratization or straightforward colonial continuity erases the choices, compromises, and coercion through which actual institutions were remade.

### What the archive can and cannot tell us
Military government records preserve orders, budgets, staffing decisions, intelligence evaluations, and correspondence. Their survival makes them indispensable but does not make the occupation's viewpoint sufficient. Reports may treat strikes as security threats without recording why workers stopped work; economic data may count rice without revealing the unequal burden of obtaining it; administrative notes may describe efficient appointments without examining popular distrust. Korean-language newspapers, local testimony, and social histories can recover different questions, although each brings its own problems of selection and rhetoric. A careful narrative asks what a source was written to accomplish before using it to characterize society.

By 1948 the military government's function had become tied to the creation of institutions for a southern state, not simply the restoration of normal administration. It remained an occupying authority until the Republic of Korea was established, but the political field had narrowed as international negotiations failed and elections proceeded in the areas accessible to UN observation. The transition therefore solved one question—how an organized government would take office in the south—while leaving the nation's territorial and ideological conflict unresolved. The significance of USAMGIK lies in that double result: state capacity was reconstructed in one zone, while the hope of a single sovereign authority across the peninsula receded.

### Why the occupation budget became constitutional politics
To administer a territory is to decide what can be taxed, purchased, subsidized, and postponed. A ministry's budget is therefore a record of priorities and of limits. Under military occupation, financial authority could remain with American officials even when Korean department heads became prominent in public. That unequal authority mattered in disputes about salaries, public services, and relief. For Korean politicians seeking meaningful self-government, the visible expansion of advisory roles could feel inadequate so long as decisive spending and security choices remained outside their control. To American administrators, retaining those powers could seem essential to avoiding abrupt collapse. These opposing assessments reveal a constitutional question concealed inside routine accounting: who was answerable to the population for the consequences of policy?

Property administration sharpened the problem. Former Japanese-owned factories and buildings were not only economic assets. They could create employment, supply housing, finance parties, and symbolize whether the colonial order had truly ended. Decisions about them could generate accusations of favoritism and corruption. Transfer of ownership required procedures whose legality might be defined by the occupation authority but whose fairness was judged by the people living with the result. This gap between lawful competence and social legitimacy explains why the question of who controlled inherited property remained so politically charged.

### The difference between a policy and a lived result
A directive issued in Seoul could be received in a provincial office with limited staff, poor information, and local alliances of its own. Implementing an economic measure might require cooperation from wholesalers who disagreed with its terms; implementing a police order might depend on officers whose conduct residents distrusted. Reform cannot be measured solely by its announcement. The historical record needs evidence of what changed outside the central office: supplies delivered, classrooms opened, property disputes decided, detainees treated, and complaints investigated. Such evidence is often fragmentary, which is a reason for caution rather than permission to substitute a plausible story for a documented outcome.

The military government also operated in a political environment becoming more polarized by the month. A measure presented as stabilizing could be interpreted as an intervention for one faction. A local protest could contain an immediate wage dispute and still be organized by activists with a national political strategy. Separating those dimensions is essential. People are capable of holding convictions and having material needs simultaneously; an official report that recognizes only one of those motives risks mischaracterizing the event. The occupation's legacy emerged from the accumulation of such encounters between institutional priorities and public expectations.

### Political inheritance after the handover
The new Republic in the south received institutions shaped by military government, colonial precedents, Korean administrative participation, and bitter political conflict. This inheritance included tools for routine governance and unsettled disputes about who should hold coercive power. It is tempting to assess those institutions solely through the later stability or instability of the South Korean state. Such hindsight can hide how limited the options seemed to participants in 1948. A government could need experienced officers while also needing to demonstrate that independence was not a change of national flag without a change of accountability.

The language of an institutional transition should not make it seem inevitable that the country would remain divided. Korean leaders continued to debate international involvement and the possibility of agreement across the line. But the apparatus of administration, law, police, and international recognition increasingly operated within one zone. Those structures produced material incentives to defend the emerging southern government and growing obstacles to a national settlement. The military government therefore belongs in the history not only as an occupying authority that ended, but as a formative system whose practices and conflicts outlasted its formal dissolution.

The occupations also affected how political newspapers and public notices could be produced and distributed. Control over licenses, paper, transport, and the surveillance of gatherings mattered to any organization trying to reach an audience. A Korean political leader might make a public case for representative institutions but discover that participation in an official advisory body exposed him to criticism from activists excluded from it. The line between cooperation and dependence was rarely clear to the people making such decisions. Institutional transition therefore needs to be studied through the circulation of information as well as the writing of decrees. The question of who could publish, demonstrate, and be heard gave concrete meaning to the larger promise of political freedom. When those channels narrowed, disputes that had begun as arguments over policy could become struggles about the right to participate in public life at all.

## IX.1.7
### Returning to a country that no longer had one political center
Exile shaped modern Korean political leadership. Activists who had spent years in China, the United States, the Soviet sphere, or other communities carried memories of repression and organizational strategies formed abroad. Liberation made return possible but did not settle whose earlier struggle entitled them to govern. A figure welcomed by cheering crowds might still confront an occupation authority unwilling to recognize a government-in-exile as the legal successor to colonial rule. Kim Gu's return alongside Provisional Government colleagues dramatized the difference between moral stature and practical control. They had supporters and a historic claim, but no automatic command over the police, army, or administrative machinery.

Political labels such as left, right, nationalist, socialist, or centrist became useful shorthand in the crowded liberation-era public sphere, but the labels could hide important disagreements. Nationalists differed on the role of foreign powers; left-wing activists disputed organization and timing; conservatives differed over how much of the colonial administrative inheritance to preserve. People might cooperate on rejecting foreign trusteeship and divide over labor organization, or agree that Korea should be unified while disagreeing profoundly on how to achieve it. The tragedy is not adequately described as a simple quarrel between two future governments that were already waiting to exist.

### Parties became instruments for deciding who spoke for Korea
A party in 1945 could provide offices, meetings, pamphlets, rallies, and access to sympathetic newspapers. It could also provide a way to turn a personal reputation into durable organization. This mattered to returning leaders, whose networks might have been impressive abroad but fragmented at home. Alliances with landowners, student activists, veterans of underground politics, teachers, and religious communities could build influence in different places. Yet organization also changed the character of public argument: once every meeting was interpreted as evidence for one faction or another, compromise became more costly and public language more absolute.

The aspiration to a national political settlement survived within this rivalry. Efforts at left-right cooperation and subsequent attempts to contact northern leaders were responses to a real fear that two occupation zones were hardening into rival states. Their limits should be studied rather than dismissed as inevitable failures. Foreign armies held decisive administrative powers; elections and trusteeship were disputed; repression and political violence undermined trust. In such an environment even sincere support for national unity did not guarantee agreement on who should administer the path toward it. The persistence of the unity claim, amid the growing impossibility of implementing it, is one of the period's defining tensions.

### The politics of personal credibility
Returning from exile did not provide a single kind of authority. Imprisonment, education, religious standing, anti-colonial military service, and underground organizing all generated different reputations. Local residents might judge a leader's promise against more immediate conditions such as food prices, police behavior, and employment. Military governments often evaluated leaders by their apparent capacity to deliver order or legitimacy. Such assessments were interested judgments, not neutral rankings of who deserved to rule. A historical account should therefore keep popular attachment, organizational strength, and administrative recognition analytically separate rather than collapse them into the word 'leadership.'

## IX.1.8
### The return journey ends at a crowded threshold
Liberation brought the movement of people as well as governments. Individuals who had worked, studied, been conscripted, or been displaced elsewhere in the Japanese empire tried to reach home. Returning did not mean finding a house, job, or family arrangement unchanged. Property could have been occupied; wages could buy less than expected; transport and health services were strained. Repatriation turned the abstract language of regained nationhood into material questions at railway stations, ports, kitchens, and local offices. The scale and uneven timing of movement made statistical summaries important, but numbers alone cannot recover what a returnee discovered upon arrival.

Colonial production had linked regions through routes and markets that did not respect the new occupation line. Northern factories and southern farms were connected by transport, finance, and trade networks that could no longer operate normally. The division of the peninsula interrupted exchanges even before separate governments were formalized. Food availability became an administrative and moral crisis: families judged officials by what could actually be bought and eaten. Rising prices eroded savings, and people dependent on fixed incomes were especially vulnerable. A government might announce plans for procurement or relief, but implementation involved traders, local officials, police, and households who did not all share its priorities.

### Food can transform political credibility
Economic hardship can be politically explosive without producing a single ideology. A farmer objecting to grain demands, a worker demanding a wage, and a returning migrant looking for housing need not join the same party. Yet their grievances could converge in a demonstration when all interpreted shortages as evidence of incompetent or unjust administration. Relief supplies could ease urgent need and simultaneously create arguments about allocation, dependency, and favoritism. The American GARIOA relief program belongs within this history of immediate consumption needs rather than being mistaken for a comprehensive development program.

The northern and southern zones encountered different industrial and agricultural legacies, but neither could escape the dislocation of empire and war. The central challenge was not merely scarcity; it was the uncertainty about which institutions were entitled to organize scarcity. A ration card, a police permit, an eviction notice, or an employer's wage schedule could make the politics of occupation unavoidable in everyday life. That is why a study of partition must include domestic labor, shelter, illness, and unpaid care as well as speeches about sovereignty.

### Ordinary people were not a single political public
Households varied by gender, age, class, place, and migration history. For some women the end of colonial restrictions opened public opportunities; for others household burden intensified as relatives returned without resources or did not return at all. People who had accumulated skills inside the colonial economy could find them useful but politically suspect. Workers in urban industry and tenants in farming districts faced different forms of leverage. Historical sources that organize people into one grateful or one angry population risk suppressing the difficult choices of these groups. Material history offers a way to see independence not as an event completed in August 1945 but as a contested redistribution of risks and opportunities.

## IX.1.9
### Strikes and the language of public order
The labor conflicts and protests of 1946 grew within a society experiencing inflation, shortages, sharp political polarization, and disputes over the legitimacy of policing. The autumn upheavals were not a single event with a single leader and uniformly agreed aims. Strikes could begin around conditions of work or public administration and draw other grievances into their orbit. Police responses could produce new demonstrations, while factional organizations attempted to shape their direction. Reports written by authorities commonly emphasize threats to public order; accounts written by participants may emphasize grievances, violence suffered, and the demand to remove officials viewed as compromised by the colonial past. Both forms require contextual reading.

The geography of the unrest is essential. A national headline can flatten a varied landscape of urban factories, transport workers, rural communities, and provincial centers. The same slogan might have a different immediate meaning where a household was short of rice and where workers feared dismissals. Participants did not necessarily know the full scope of events unfolding elsewhere. Nor were all protests peaceful, or every act of repression justified by the existence of violent incidents. Reconstructing sequence—what occurred before police intervention, what followed it, and which evidence survives—matters more than assigning collective guilt to an entire political camp.

### Why administrative trust broke down
A government with little popular legitimacy may interpret protest principally as disobedience. Demonstrators, in turn, may interpret a police unit as the continuation of familiar colonial authority even when its formal employer has changed. This reciprocal suspicion helps explain how practical disputes escalated. It does not excuse violence against civilians or erase differences among local circumstances. The critical historical problem is how political claims became inseparable from force: who could assemble, who could strike, who could arrest, and who would investigate deaths. Each answer shaped the meaning of postcolonial citizenship before a new constitution was written.

The events of autumn 1946 also warn against using later Cold War categories as an automatic explanation for every participant's motive. Communist organizations mattered, as did anti-communist repression and the politics of occupation. But many people experienced the crisis through wages, food, police conduct, and local reputation. A responsible reader should distinguish organized political strategy from the full set of grievances that gave demonstrations social depth. A historical narrative that is too tidy in its assignment of heroes and enemies is likely to be too tidy about the evidence.

### Memory survives unevenly
Violence can produce contradictory archives. Security forces document threats and arrests; parties commemorate martyrs; family memories preserve losses that never entered an official file. Later states may discourage certain memories and elevate others. The role of scholarship is not to combine these materials into an easy average. It is to ask what each source can show, where testimony conflicts, and which questions remain unanswered. That method preserves the distinction between documented event, plausible interpretation, and rhetoric employed afterward. The autumn uprisings belong to the larger history of state-building because they reveal how early administrative decisions affected the boundary between authorized politics and punishable dissent.

## IX.1.10
### An independence settlement negotiated without agreement on sovereignty
The December 1945 Moscow meeting produced a plan associated with a provisional Korean government and a possible period of trusteeship. The proposals were debated in a political atmosphere in which people had just escaped decades of colonial rule and were unwilling to imagine another externally supervised future. Yet opposition to trusteeship was not simply a disagreement about vocabulary. It revealed different assumptions about which Korean organizations could legitimately negotiate, how the occupying powers should be involved, and whether international oversight would advance or delay independence. Public reporting of the Moscow decisions changed the domestic political balance, helping reorganize alliances and intensify conflict.

The proposed US–Soviet Joint Commission was meant to provide a mechanism for consultation. In practice, the two occupying powers differed over which Korean groups should be consulted and how to interpret acceptable political participation. The dispute was not merely bureaucratic: the decision about whom to invite into the room affected who might become the future government. A conference table could therefore become another arena of the struggle for sovereignty. The procedural deadlock also gave local political groups reasons to mobilize, accuse rivals of betrayal, and seek support outside Korea.

### Why trusteeship divided rather than united
The term trusteeship could be heard as an insult to the competence and sacrifice of people who had worked for independence. Opponents used its apparent contradiction with liberation to mobilize support. Others emphasized the larger Moscow framework and the possibility of a unified provisional government. Their arguments were often translated into absolute categories of patriotism and disloyalty, although policy positions and personal motives were more complicated. It is important not to repeat the claim that every opponent of trusteeship had one political allegiance or that every advocate of cooperation endorsed indefinite foreign control. The divisions emerged from competing interpretations of a proposed pathway to independence within an already divided occupation.

The bitter debates weakened possibilities for compromise, but domestic polarization alone cannot explain their outcome. The United States and Soviet Union pursued different strategic interests and operated separate occupation systems that were becoming more entrenched. Korean parties and leaders acted within those constraints without being mere puppets. The failure of cooperation thus reflects both international competition and local political conflict. Assigning exclusive responsibility to one side may satisfy a retrospective national narrative, but it risks excluding the very evidence needed to understand why proposed solutions failed.

### Korea enters the United Nations
As bilateral negotiations stalled, the United States brought the Korean question to the United Nations in 1947. The UN General Assembly adopted proposals for an electoral process and international observation. Here the language of self-determination encountered a basic limitation: the commission was unable to operate throughout the peninsula because the Soviet authorities did not permit its access to the northern zone. The resulting process in the south could create representative institutions for the territory in which it operated, but it could not by itself secure agreement on a unified peninsula-wide government. The jurisdictional limit was political rather than merely geographic.

The UN Yearbook and contemporary U.S. State Department documents illuminate the international framing of the process. They should be read beside Korean-language histories of local mobilization, opposition, and alternative proposals. International records can tell us who proposed a resolution and how votes were cast; they cannot on their own explain what a village resident expected from a ballot, or why a particular Korean organization rejected the process. The distinction between the institutional validity of a procedure and its capacity to settle a national conflict runs through the whole story.

### Two governments and an unresolved national question
In 1948 a Republic of Korea government was established in the south, followed by a Democratic People's Republic of Korea government in the north. Each drew on institutions built during occupation and advanced its own account of national legitimacy. These proclamations did not restore a single sovereign system covering the peninsula. They gave the dividing line a more durable governmental form and made rival claims about representation harder to reconcile. The result was not simply a border imposed once in 1945, nor was it the unavoidable expression of two eternally separate communities. It was a process in which military jurisdiction, administrative development, global rivalry, local ideology, social conflict, and political decisions reinforced one another.

The temptation is to make 1948 an ending because governments have names and formal dates. For people crossing the peninsula, separated from family, waiting for legal recognition, or hoping that negotiations might resume, it was not an ending. Questions about land, property, citizenship, security, and national belonging continued. The later outbreak of war cannot be read back into every decision as though the outcome were already known. Understanding the failure of a unified settlement requires restoring the alternatives that participants still imagined, while acknowledging that those alternatives were increasingly constrained by institutions and violence. Liberation was real; so was the tragedy that independence and division took shape together.
