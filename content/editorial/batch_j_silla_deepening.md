# Batch J — Append-Only Silla Village Studies

These additions follow the earlier articles, without revising their existing paragraphs.

## IV.3.1
### Counting dependent workers without erasing them
The Silla village register preserves enslaved persons within its age and sex categories. The National Institute of Korean History's transcription of the village document makes this more than a generalized claim about hierarchy: a named settlement's count includes people identified as no or bi. Those labels should not be translated into a uniform modern experience of slavery, because their precise legal and economic circumstances may have varied. Yet neither should the people concerned vanish into the arithmetic of the register. The administration assessed them as part of the productive and dependent population; it did not record how they negotiated daily life, family ties or survival. This contrast between unusually concrete counting and severe loss of personal testimony is a central limit of the Silla social archive. It also clarifies the relationship between social rank and labor. Political privilege at the capital was sustained through many household relationships whose coercive dimensions rarely became a subject of royal narrative.

### Rank can conceal regional variation
The familiar bone-rank system captures high-level Silla aristocratic difference, but it is an unsuitable universal key for every person in every locality. A village leader, a temple worker and an elite relative at court did not necessarily hold power through the same institution. Later sources sometimes describe ranks in systematic terms, but those descriptions need to be compared against documents and material settings of the period under study. It is also wrong to assume that a person not recorded by name lacked social influence in their own community. Local knowledge of fields, kin and routes mattered in practice, even when formal status sharply constrained legal agency. The history of hierarchy needs both the central vocabulary of rank and the provincial evidence of assessment, labor and unequal resource control.

## IV.3.3
### Four villages, not four thousand
It matters that the famous village register documents four specific communities near Seowongyeong rather than a representative sample of the whole peninsula. Historians can analyze variations within its columns but cannot straightforwardly extrapolate its household counts to the entire realm. The document's date and the meaning of several field categories remain debated. This is not a reason to discard it: few other Silla materials bring people, animals, fields and trees into such immediate contact. It is a reason to explain the source's geographic and textual limits whenever figures appear. A village could combine paddy cultivation, dry fields and tree resources in proportions quite different from another district. Residence and household classifications also reflected state purposes. The document gives us a detailed administrative portrait, not an eyewitness account of every ordinary exchange. Its value rests in holding those truths together.

### Rural society had a political memory
When kings conquered territory or redirected taxes, provincial communities had to respond within older networks of kinship, exchange and local authority. The written archive privileges declarations and court crises, but an order did not implement itself. Someone had to measure a field, relay a demand, count an ox and settle a dispute over who would provide transport. Local intermediaries could make state commands effective, reinterpret them or become targets of complaint. The village register supplies traces of such implementation without naming each intermediary. This makes rural society a necessary component of political history. The state reached villages through people already embedded there; administration was a relationship that had to be reproduced rather than a simple line drawn on a map.

## IV.3.4
### The evidence for rice is more complicated than a rice paddy photograph
The village register distinguishes wet rice fields, dry fields and other categories, and the National Institute of Korean History discusses the variation in their measured areas. Some recorded villages had unusually substantial paddy areas, but even in the small surviving sample this distribution was not identical across localities. A modern image of flooded fields cannot tell us the exact cultivation calendar or labor organization behind every historical measurement. Fields required water control and maintenance, while dry crops, livestock and trees gave households additional resources. The document supports the historical importance of wet cultivation in certain communities. It does not prove the yield per hectare, a single kingdom-wide crop regime or uniform property rights. Interpreting this evidence demands attention to terrain, historical measures and the institution responsible for recording the fields.

### Water and work in a landscape of risk
Farming was an ecological negotiation conducted by people with unequal capacity to absorb loss. A damaged channel, exhausted soil or missing work animal could change the production available from land that still appeared on an official list. Local cooperation might be essential for repairing waterways, but that did not erase differences in rank, land access or obligations. Livestock counted in the register could contribute to cultivation and movement without serving a single purpose everywhere. Historical agricultural explanation is strongest when it joins material possibility to the partial record of administration. It should neither romanticize peaceful communal labor nor imagine a fully measurable productivity system where no continuous series survives.
