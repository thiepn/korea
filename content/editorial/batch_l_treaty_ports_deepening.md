# Batch L — Append-only treaty-port and mission context

## VIII.1.1
### Why a clause must be read with its consequences
The Treaty of Ganghwa is remembered for legal changes that reorganized the relation between Japan and Joseon. Yet the significance of each provision developed through subsequent use. Permission to open a port was not identical to the community that developed there, and language about jurisdiction had consequences only through officials and residents who invoked it. Treaty reading therefore benefits from comparison with later trade arrangements and records of dispute. An official statement of equality between states could coexist with unequal bargaining power, while the ability of Japanese subjects to claim foreign legal protection complicated the exercise of Korean local authority. We should distinguish what the text directly stipulated, what negotiators expected, and what later events made possible. That distinction enables readers to move from this foundational document to its commercial and diplomatic aftermath without pretending that the entire later history was contained in the signature of 1876.

## VIII.1.2
### The ports connected two unequal kinds of mobility
Busan and Wonsan were not merely international entry points; they were places where the mobility of foreign traders met the obligations of local communities. Residents worked as carriers, cleaners, sellers, translators, and boat operators, supplying the infrastructure of port life while facing rules of jurisdiction that could differ from those applied to foreign nationals. The effects of trade moved inland through routes already used by Korean merchants. Not every household prospered or lost in the same way. What was new was the concentration of legal privilege, shipping access, commercial information, and official attention around particular urban spaces. Comparing port residents' lived conditions with the wording of treaties can reveal how sovereignty became a practical question. This continuation adds social and legal context without changing the earlier description of either town.

## VIII.1.5
### Returning home was the beginning of comparison
A Korean envoy's return from Japan did not settle the question of which foreign practices could be adopted. Reports were read by a court divided over the costs and risks of change, and a visitor's observations could support competing policy arguments. A factory demonstrated capacity without disclosing every condition that sustained it; a school appeared instructive without necessarily providing a model affordable or politically acceptable at home. Kim Hong-jip's encounter with Huang Zunxian's Joseon chaekryak in 1880 also shows how travel allowed a foreign policy argument to enter Korean domestic controversy. The mission's importance lies not only in the sights recorded but also in the translation of observation into proposals, institutional experiments, and vigorous opposition. Those afterlives belong beside the itinerary itself.
