## V.1.2
### The injunctions as inherited advice and as evidence
The Ten Injunctions associated with Taejo are often read as direct instructions carried faithfully from founder to successor. Their transmission through later historical writing makes that confidence difficult to sustain without qualification. They are also valuable as a record of what later generations believed the foundation ought to teach. Advice about religious institutions, regional considerations and dynastic continuity made the founder an authority invoked in disputes that occurred long after his death. Reading the injunctions alongside the histories of succession and Seogyeong shows why political memory was more than reverence: an argument attributed to Taejo could influence what a later king thought legitimate. This is contextual analysis, not a claim that each surviving formulation can be authenticated word for word to 943.

### Family policy remained a source of future conflict
A royal marriage might confirm the allegiance of an important house, but it also gave that house an investment in its descendants' future. The foundation's celebrated inclusiveness could therefore become a structural succession difficulty once Taejo's personal prestige was gone. An official recognizing the monarchy might still distinguish sharply between possible heirs. Hyejong's and Jeongjong's brief reigns indicate how uncertain those relationships remained. The story adds a complication to the conventional contrast between an accommodating founder and forceful successors: compromise was indispensable to winning the realm, but every compromise could produce claims that later kings had to manage without the founder's authority.

## V.1.5
### The examination made merit visible but not equally accessible
The examination system associated with Gwangjong and Ssang Gi in 958 transformed the language of public recruitment. It enabled a ruler to point to assessed literary skill rather than relying exclusively on kinship, regional service, or personal recommendation. Yet preparing for examinations required material conditions—books, teaching, time and the ability to remain connected to official society. People with those resources could convert the new rules into durable family advantage. The system's significance was therefore not a democratic opening of every office. It was the development of a formalized career channel within a highly unequal society, a channel that rulers and elite families alike could use.

### A scholar-official's authority still needed a province
An official who could recite texts and compose polished answers was not automatically able to govern a district he had never visited. Knowledge of fields, transport, customary obligations and local disputes still rested in communities and intermediaries. The strengthening of learned recruitment under Gwangjong and Seongjong must therefore be connected to provincial centers and the appointment of local officials. Examinations helped explain why a king considered someone eligible to govern; provincial institutions determined whether that eligibility could be turned into effective action. The two developments were complementary answers to the problem first encountered after 936: how can authority depend on repeatable procedures rather than only on personal loyalty?
