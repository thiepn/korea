# Batch G — Added evidence questions in the completed VI.4 and IX.3 collections

## VI.4.5
### Where design descriptions meet historical sound change
The explanatory tradition of Hunminjeongeum remains most useful when it is allowed to disagree with a modern classroom diagram. An original consonant category may resemble a modern grouping but still represent a distinction whose phonetic value has shifted. The famous association between letter shape and speech organs therefore cannot replace comparative study of fifteenth-century spellings, transcribed forms, rhyme patterns and loanword evidence. Every tool illuminates a different angle. A royal explanation tells us about intellectual design; spelling in a surviving text reveals an attempt to represent particular speech; comparison with later records exposes change rather than an error in the earlier system.

Even the survival of the explanatory document requires historical accounting. A text we consult in print or digital facsimile has passed through preservation, rediscovery, editorial transcription and modern scholarly interpretation. The comfortable sense that we can look straight into Sejong's workshop should be resisted. What remains extraordinary is not unfettered access to the inventors' private process, but an unusually explicit articulation of principles that can be tested against other linguistic evidence.

## VI.4.6
### The page as a technological constraint
A syllabic block is a flexible visual unit, but its flexibility has a practical cost: printers must have or make workable forms for multiple combinations. When the script moved among brush writing, carved blocks and movable type, the available technologies shaped how pages were prepared and read. It is tempting to treat the script's systematic nature as automatically making production simple. In fact, a rational writing system still needs compositors, proofreaders, reusable conventions and materials. The relative ease of learning a sign and the relative ease of manufacturing a book are separate questions.

This distinction also changes how we imagine the circulation of the new letters. The appearance of a royal text proves that a workshop and its patrons could produce that text. It does not tell us how many copies entered ordinary homes, what portion of their audience could read unassisted, or whether the same spellings were used outside the circle that sponsored the book. Typographic achievement and social diffusion were connected but not interchangeable.

## VI.4.7
### Opposition was neither universal nor static
Elite criticism of a new script is sometimes narrated as a unanimous campaign against literacy. The surviving opposition associated with figures such as Choe Malli should be treated more precisely. Arguments could concern diplomatic propriety, the prestige of classical Chinese, the relationship between scholarly training and state service, and whether a court should reshape established habits. Disagreement among officials does not prove that every scholar shared the same view or that critics imagined the later mass use of Hangul. Their immediate political world was much narrower.

It is also risky to infer an uninterrupted line from a fifteenth-century petition to the later fortunes of Korean writing. The status of Hangul changed in different settings over centuries. Women, religious publishers, bureaucrats, translators and popular readers did not all encounter it under identical conditions. A source that documents an elite dispute is strong evidence for the dispute; it cannot stand alone as a census of society's attitudes.

## VI.4.8
### Royal poetry and the problem of its audience
Yongbieocheonga is often celebrated as an early work in the newly created writing system, yet reading its language solely as a triumph of popular accessibility misses its dynastic setting. Royal verse could praise ancestors, organize memory and place the new dynasty within a wider moral and cosmological order. Its vernacular form mattered, but the work's sponsorship, audience and ceremonial uses also shaped what its words were expected to accomplish. The script became a medium for statecraft rather than a purely private or informal alternative.

For a modern reader, the contrast between a phonographic writing system and highly political content is productive. Forms that make language visible in new ways do not guarantee that the resulting literature will speak on behalf of people excluded from power. The same innovation can serve educational possibilities and royal legitimacy. The first century of Hangul is richer when those apparently competing purposes remain in view.

## VI.4.9
### Translation is interpretation
A Buddhist text translated or rendered for Korean readers does more than substitute one set of syllables for another. Translators must decide how to express technical religious language, whether to preserve a learned loanword, and how much commentary an audience needs to make sense of unfamiliar concepts. The early vernacular projects associated with royal and religious patronage thus produced choices with intellectual consequences. Their significance lies not only in the presence of Korean letters on the page, but in the effort to make complex teachings comprehensible across linguistic conventions.

The survival of a translation also asks us to identify its material route. Who commissioned it, who read the original, who prepared the Korean explanation, who cut or set the text, and who distributed copies? Sometimes the source answers several of these questions; often it leaves important gaps. Resisting a single heroic narrative of immediate universal education allows the translation workshop to emerge as a distinct historical institution.

## VI.4.10
### A standard never stops moving
Dongguk Jeongun belongs to efforts to consider pronunciation and proper sound at a moment when the invention of Hangul made finer distinctions visible in writing. But a normative work reflects an aspiration to order, not necessarily the everyday habits of all speakers. The distance between prescription and practice is itself a subject for historical investigation. Spellings that appear inconsistent to later standardizers may preserve meaningful differences of region, time, scribal training or genre.

The longer development of Korean orthography cannot be compressed into a story of gradual progress toward the present. Features disappear, conventions are reformulated, and readers adopt new habits under different institutions. To respect the early scholarly project is also to avoid putting modern spelling rules into the mouths of fifteenth-century compilers. Their problem was real, but it was not identical to ours.

## IX.3.5
### The winter campaign beyond command arrows
A military map of Chinese intervention can make the winter of 1950 seem like a few arrows pushing another set of arrows south. What the map conceals is the interaction of cold, roads, equipment and the limits of intelligence. A retreating unit and a family attempting to flee through the same mountain route did not inhabit the same strategic language. One had an operational order; the other might know only that a previously expected front had moved with terrifying speed. Understanding the retreat means reconstructing those different experiences without inventing specific witnesses.

Chinese entry also complicated the war's political vocabulary. Opponents described intentions through propaganda and strategic expectation, while commanders faced a changing opponent they had not fully anticipated. Later certainty should not be projected onto the decisions made before the scale of intervention was clear. The episode asks what could be known at the time, not simply which side later claimed to have predicted events.

## IX.3.6
### A static front did not mean an inactive society
The image of opposing trench lines describes only one part of the Korean War after the great movements of 1950 and early 1951. Men rotated through positions, prisoners were counted and reclassified, roads and supply points were attacked, and civilians confronted repeated choices about farming, shelter and movement near zones of danger. Attrition could be measured in casualties and artillery expenditure; its wider cost involved lost harvests, interrupted schooling, separated households and damage to infrastructure that could not be repaired by a ceasefire announcement.

Negotiations did not suspend violence. The front line had become politically consequential, and each side could interpret local military pressure as a bargaining instrument. A chronological reader should therefore resist dividing the war into 'fighting' and 'talks' as consecutive periods. The two processes overlapped in ways that shaped the armistice and the human experience of its delay.

## IX.3.7
### Air power as a perspective problem
Air-campaign statistics describe sorties, targets and losses, yet they depend on categories that do not necessarily correspond to how bombardment was experienced on the ground. A bridge appears as a logistics objective to one planner and as a lifeline connecting villages and markets to somebody else. Strategic justification and civilian consequence need to be examined separately; neither can be read directly out of the other's sources. Where casualty data are incomplete, a careful account specifies that uncertainty rather than supplying false precision.

The air war also reminds us that the conflict was international without being experienced in the same way by all participants. Pilots, anti-aircraft crews, railway workers and displaced families stood in very different relations to the same aircraft. Integrating their perspectives requires different kinds of evidence, including records produced in Korean and Chinese rather than only English-language operational histories.

## IX.3.8
### Civilian testimony changes the unit of history
An operational narrative asks when a force crossed a river or secured a road. Civilian history asks what became of the people who used that road, how families learned that a relative was missing, and why a shelter ceased to be safe. Survivors' testimonies, local investigations and family records can overturn the impression of impersonal movement produced by a campaign map. Such evidence should be handled with attention to memory, translation and the unequal opportunities people had to record loss.

No single atrocity or displacement story can represent every community on the peninsula. Allegations require careful identification of place, perpetrators, time, victims and the evidence for each claim; the war involved violence committed by more than one side. The purpose of this caution is not to blur moral responsibility. It is to keep accountability attached to acts that can be described and investigated rather than to sweeping claims unsupported by the available record.

## IX.3.9
### A prisoner could be a diplomatic dispute and a person
The repatriation dispute became one of the most difficult features of armistice negotiations because captivity joined ideology, sovereignty and personal choice. Records describe camps and categories of prisoners, but classification could be contested. Some prisoners feared return, some were subject to pressure, and authorities did not always agree on what counted as a voluntary decision. Treating the issue as a technical clause to be settled by negotiators misses how much depended on individuals whose futures could not be reduced to military arithmetic.

Humanitarian institutions produced a different documentary trail from combat commands: access requests, visits, lists, communications with families and attempts to establish conditions. These records have their own limitations and institutional viewpoints. Together they can clarify the process without claiming that the viewpoint of a camp administrator, an international delegate and a captive were necessarily identical.

## IX.3.10
### An armistice has a narrow legal achievement
The July 1953 armistice established military procedures for halting hostilities; it did not conclude a comprehensive peace settlement between all competing political claims. Its terms therefore deserve careful reading rather than being treated as a decorative closing date. Maps and demarcation procedures affected real communities, and institutions charged with supervision operated within an environment of deep distrust. The agreement was consequential precisely because it created a functioning halt to open warfare without resolving the reasons the war had begun.

The persistence of division should not make the achievement seem trivial. Families could survive because bombardment and battlefield fighting ceased on a large scale, even while displacement and separation continued. The responsible historical distinction is between what the text formally provided, what the parties implemented, and what later publics wanted it to mean. Only by separating those questions can readers understand why 1953 was both an ending and the beginning of another long chapter.
