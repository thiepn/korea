# Batch K — Preserved-Text Extensions for Existing Gojoseon Articles

## II.1.2
### The written story has its own date
The most familiar Dangun account survives in the medieval Samguk yusa. This date is essential to source criticism: the story may draw on earlier traditions, but the surviving written witness does not give us an eyewitness account of a founder acting in 2333 BCE. Elements of the narrative—the heaven-descended figure, the transformed bear, and an ethical vision of rule—were available for later communities to interpret in many ways. They should be understood as evidence for a powerful tradition of remembrance, not simply as material to be either accepted or rejected as a modern field report. Readers can honor the cultural importance of the foundation story while asking precisely how and when its surviving wording reached us. The same distinction applies to references in the Jewang ungi and in Joseon-era rituals that commemorated ancient founders. Such practices show what later people valued and institutionalized. They cannot on their own settle the chronology of the ancient polity.

## II.1.4
### Excavation context is more than an artifact label
A dagger may be photographed in isolation, but the archaeological information lies in its context: associated pottery, grave form, stratigraphy, manufacturing technique, and relationship to comparable sites. The Liaoning-type bronze dagger is particularly prominent in debates over early Joseon, yet no individual blade can identify a whole political territory by itself. Material styles can spread through imitation, trade, marriage networks or the movement of skilled people. Conversely, different burial practices can coexist among communities linked by political authority. A useful comparison asks which combinations of features occur together, how confidently they are dated, and whether discoveries cluster around particular routes. Even rich tombs can tell us more securely about social differentiation and commemorative display than about a named official title. Archaeological evidence becomes more convincing when a claim is limited to what the excavation actually supports rather than expanded automatically into a dynastic geography.

## II.1.7
### Resources connecting economy and early political power
Agriculture, craftwork and exchange were not passive backdrops to Gojoseon's political story. Communities relied on stored food, repairable tools, control of routes, and networks capable of moving valuable objects. Leaders seeking durable authority would have needed access to these resources, whether through reciprocal obligation, patronage, negotiated tribute or coercion. We should not infer a standardized royal tax code from an excavated storehouse or from the presence of prestige goods. Such finds demonstrate activity; their legal and institutional meaning must be argued separately. The economy also worked unevenly across regions. River valleys, coastal routes and uplands offered differing opportunities, while weather and political conflict could disrupt a household's ability to maintain stores. Archaeological traces provide the strongest direct evidence for production; transmitted political narratives occasionally show what elites sought to control. Putting the two types of source in conversation helps restore the practical foundations of authority without fabricating a complete bureaucratic system.
